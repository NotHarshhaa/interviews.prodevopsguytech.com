# Advanced Level

Senior interviews are scenario-driven: "design this", "you're on call when...", "how have you led...". Answers are evaluated on **trade-off reasoning, operational scars, and organizational thinking**. Each entry below gives a model answer structure, the follow-ups that probe depth, and the pitfall that sinks senior candidates.

<Steps>
<Step>
**How would you secure a Kubernetes cluster from scratch?**

**Model answer structure:** layered defense, named per layer — then verified:

1. **Control plane & identity** — managed control plane where possible; RBAC with least-privilege Roles (no cluster-admin for humans), OIDC-federated auth, no automounted tokens where unneeded
2. **Admission enforcement** — Pod Security Standards (`restricted`) or Kyverno/Gatekeeper policies: no privileged pods, pinned images, mandatory probes/limits; only signed images admitted (cosign verification)
3. **Network** — default-deny NetworkPolicies east-west, explicit allows per service path, mTLS between services via mesh
4. **Workload hardening** — non-root, read-only rootfs, dropped capabilities, seccomp
5. **Secrets** — External Secrets/Vault sync, etcd encryption, RBAC on Secret reads
6. **Supply chain & runtime** — scanned images (Trivy gate), SBOMs, Falco-style runtime detection, audit logging shipped to the SIEM

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "Which single control catches the most real attacks?" — misconfiguration prevention (admission policy) — most breaches are config, not kernel exploits
- "How do you keep it from blocking developers?" — advisory rollout first, golden-path templates that are compliant by default
- "How do you prove it's working?" — test pods (can I reach the DB directly?), policy audit reports, periodic pentest

**Common pitfall:** listing controls without the enforcement/verification story — a control you can't prove is a control you don't have.
</Step>

<Step>
**You're on call: production is down, users are erroring. Walk me through your first 30 minutes.**

**Model answer structure:**

1. **Declare** — declare an incident, assign an incident commander (separate from the person debugging), start a timeline and a comms channel
2. **Assess** — what changed last? (deploy annotations on dashboards, recent config/infra changes) — the majority of outages follow a change
3. **Mitigate first** — rollback or feature-flag-off beats root-cause debugging: stability before curiosity. Verify recovery on user-facing metrics, not server logs alone
4. **Communicate** — status page and stakeholder updates on a cadence, even when the update is "still investigating"
5. **Only then** — root-cause: traces → logs → the specific hop
6. **Close** — blameless post-mortem within days: timeline, contributing causes, action items with owners

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What if rollback is impossible?" — irreversible schema change: forward-fix behind review, degrade gracefully (feature flags), communicate honestly
- "How do you separate hypothesis-testing from thrashing?" — one change at a time, measured against user-facing metrics, IC gates changes
- "What's in your post-mortem?" — timeline, contributing causes (plural, systemic), action items with owners/dates, and what monitoring would have caught it earlier

**Common pitfall:** heroics — the IC/debugger split and mitigation-first discipline are exactly what they're testing at senior level.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you roll out a risky change to production?**

**Model answer structure:** progressive delivery, with the decision split between machines and humans:

1. **Ship dark** — deploy with the feature flag off; zero user exposure while it soaks in production
2. **Internal cohort** — employees/dogfooders use it; real traffic paths exercised
3. **Canary with metric gates** — 1–5% of traffic; automated analysis of error/latency deltas vs baseline over a bake window; regression → auto-rollback
4. **Expand on evidence** — 25% → 50% → 100%, gates at each step
5. **Retire the flag** — on a schedule; flags are debt

Underneath: proven rollback (tested), backward-compatible schemas/APIs (old and new coexist), and observability that can actually distinguish versions.

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What metrics gate the canary?" — error-rate delta, p99 latency, saturation vs the baseline cohort; business KPIs for product changes
- "Who decides to expand?" — automation on technical gates; humans on business gates
- "How do you handle non-idempotent or schema-touching changes?" — expand/contract discipline first

**Common pitfall:** a canary without metric analysis — "deploy to 5% and hope" is not progressive delivery.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you scale monitoring and observability for 50+ microservices?**

**Model answer structure:**

- **Standardize instrumentation** — OpenTelemetry across all services (metrics/logs/traces with semantic conventions); a collector pipeline for processing (sampling, PII scrubbing, enrichment)
- **Structure** — Prometheus per cluster with a global layer (Thanos/Mimir) for long retention and cross-cluster queries; Loki or Elasticsearch for logs with per-team tenant limits; trace backend with exemplar links to logs
- **Governance** — golden dashboards templated per service (a new service inherits RED dashboards automatically via repo template); cardinality policy enforced in instrumentation review; per-team ingestion quotas
- **Alerting** — SLOs per service, burn-rate alerting routed to owning teams, meta-monitoring + watchdog for the monitoring stack itself
- **Cost** — retention tiers per data class, sampling for traces, cardinality and verbosity as reviewed decisions

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "One team's cardinality explosion degrades everyone — what do you do?" — per-tenant limits, collector-side drops, and the review process that caught it
- "How do teams onboard?" — golden-path templates, not central-team ticket queues
- "What do you cut when cost pressure hits?" — retention tiers, trace sampling, debug verbosity — ranked by value-per-GB

**Common pitfall:** central-team-does-everything designs — the scalable answer is platform + golden paths + team ownership.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you handle a required, non-rollback-able database schema migration on a live system?**

**Model answer structure:** this is where expand/contract proves itself — and if it wasn't followed, the answer is honest triage:

- **If designed correctly** — the migration is additive and backward-compatible: apply it, verify, deploy the code that uses it; the "rollback" is deploying the old code, which still works
- **If it isn't backward-compatible** — options in order of preference: (1) forward-fix with a corrective migration behind feature flags; (2) restore snapshot into a parallel instance, cut over after reconciliation (accepting data loss between snapshot and now — quantify it for the business); (3) data-reconciliation scripts post-rollback
- **The rehearsal** — this exact scenario against a production-scale copy before touching prod, with timing measured
- **The prevention** — destructive migrations never coupled to app deploys; expand/contract as enforced convention; backups with tested *restore* (restore speed is the forgotten constraint)

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "Who decides to accept data loss?" — business owner, with the engineer quantifying the window — not the on-call alone
- "How do you avoid this situation?" — migration review gate in CI: any non-backward-compatible change requires a staged rollout plan

**Common pitfall:** confidently describing "flyway undo" — senior interviewers know it's a paid feature and that undo rarely covers real cases.
</Step>

<Step>
**How would you reduce a team's change failure rate?**

**Model answer structure:** treat it as a diagnosis before a prescription — high failure rate has a small number of usual causes:

- **Deploying too big** — batch size drives risk: smaller, more frequent changes bisect easier; trunk-based development with flags
- **Missing gates** — add the test classes that match the failures: flaky E2E replaced by contract tests, schema-compat checks for DB changes, load tests for performance regressions
- **No progressive exposure** — canaries/flags catch at 5% instead of 100%
- **Human process gaps** — no review, Friday-evening deploys, skipped runbooks — process and guardrails, not blame
- **Measure the effect** — DORA metrics trending: failure rate down *while* frequency rises is the healthy signature; failure rate down because deploys stopped is a hidden regression

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "Leadership wants deploys stopped until failures stop — your response?" — the evidence-based counter: freezing change *increases* risk per release; double down on progressive delivery instead
- "What if the failures are all one service?" — targeted ownership/gate fix, not team-wide process change

**Common pitfall:** answering with "more testing" alone — the DORA-informed, batch-size-first reasoning is the senior signature.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you design disaster recovery for a platform?**

**Model answer structure:** start from business numbers, not technology:

1. **RTO/RPO per service tier** — the business states tolerable downtime and data loss; these choose the strategy, not the engineer's preference
2. **Strategy per tier** — Tier 1: multi-region active-active or warm standby (minutes RTO, near-zero RPO); Tier 2: pilot light; Tier 3: backup & restore (hours)
3. **The data layer is the work** — cross-region replication (async lag = RPO), global databases or reconciliation, backup policies with retention
4. **Buildability** — every environment rebuildable from IaC + Git; etcd/cluster snapshots; images in registries
5. **Testing** — scheduled failover drills (GameDays) with timing measured against RTO; restore drills against production-scale data; untested DR is a hypothesis
6. **The forgotten pieces** — DNS/TTL failover mechanics, external dependencies' DR (your provider's outage is yours), secrets/keys replicated, and the runbook that works at 3 a.m.

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What does a drill look like?" — time the full failover, find the gaps, file them — repeat quarterly
- "Backup vs DR?" — backup answers data loss; DR answers system availability — you need both, and restore *speed* is part of both

**Common pitfall:** describing tooling without RTO/RPO-driven tiers and tested drills — the business framing is the senior marker.
</Step>

<Step>
**How have you driven cultural adoption of DevOps practices in an organization that resisted?**

**Model answer structure (STAR format — have a real story):**

- **Situation** — siloed teams, manual releases, fear of change; specific friction you observed
- **Task** — your mandate and the constraints (no budget for big-bang reorg, deadlines continuing)
- **Action** — the sequence that worked: pilot team with the worst manual pain → automate *their* flow → publish their DORA improvements → golden-path templates so other teams inherit wins → blameless post-mortems replacing finger-pointing → platform team as enablers, not gatekeepers
- **Result** — concrete numbers: lead time from weeks to days, deploy frequency, incident rate; and the cultural marker — teams *asking* for the pipeline instead of resisting it

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What failed along the way?" — have one; pretending everything worked reads as inexperience
- "How did you handle the loudest skeptic?" — usually by making their worst task automated; specific example expected
- "What would you do differently?" — self-awareness is the actual test

**Common pitfall:** tool-centric stories — senior interviewers are assessing organizational influence; every good answer names people and incentives, not just pipelines.
</Step>

<Step>
**Design the CI/CD and infrastructure for a new product from zero. Walk me through your decisions.**

**Model answer structure:** reason in order of constraints:

1. **Requirements first** — team size, expected traffic shape, compliance needs, cloud choice/existing estate — these drive everything
2. **Foundation** — IaC from day one (Terraform, remote state, per-env dirs), Git repo structure, one cloud account per env (landing zone guardrails)
3. **Pipeline** — build once → test → artifact (container image) → promote dev→staging→prod; PR gates (lint, unit, integration, SCA/SAST); prod behind approvals; deploy strategy = rolling with readiness gates, canary when risk warrants
4. **Runtime** — Kubernetes if the team can operate it (managed), managed services for data tier, secrets via OIDC + vault, least-privilege IAM
5. **Observability from day one** — OTel instrumentation, SLOs agreed with product, symptom-based alerting, dashboards templated
6. **Security woven in** — policy-as-code, signed images, header/TLS hygiene — enforced by pipeline, not wiki
7. **Cost & scale honesty** — start boring and small; autoscaling and the big-architecture decisions wait for measured load

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What would you deliberately NOT build yet?" — the restraint answer: multi-region, exotic HA, microservices — until load and team size demand them
- "How do you keep it maintainable by the team after you?" — golden paths, docs-as-code, boring technology choices

**Common pitfall:** over-architecting for imagined scale — the best senior answers are aggressively boring and constraint-driven.
</Step>

<Step>
**Tell me about the worst outage or incident you've handled.**

**Model answer structure (STAR — this tests honesty, learning, and calm):**

- **Situation/Impact** — scope and duration stated plainly, with numbers
- **The compounding factors** — why it was hard: cascading failure, missing observability, an irreversible component
- **Actions** — mitigation sequence, IC discipline, what finally resolved it
- **The learning** — the systemic fixes that followed (monitoring gap closed, runbook written, architecture change), stated as implemented-and-verified rather than "lessons were learned"
- **Honesty markers** — name your own mistake in the chain if there was one; blameless framing of others

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What would you do differently now?" — specific, implementable changes
- "How did you communicate during it?" — cadence, honesty about unknowns, stakeholder management
- "Did it ever nearly happen again? What caught it?" — the verification that the fix works

**Common pitfall:** a story with no personal accountability or no systemic fix — either reads as immaturity. A real scar with a real fix is the winning answer.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you keep platform/infrastructure costs under control without hurting delivery?**

**Model answer structure:** governance + engineering levers, ordered by impact:

- **Visibility first** — mandatory tagging (enforced by policy), cost per team/service dashboards beside latency; untagged = unowned
- **Kill waste** — rightsizing from utilization data, scheduled non-prod scale-down, zombie resources (unattached volumes, idle LBs) — usually the first 20–30% found
- **Pricing model alignment** — commitments on measured baselines, Spot for interruptible work, autoscaling both directions
- **Architecture levers** — CDN offload, storage lifecycle tiers, managed services vs self-managed math, egress-aware design (cross-AZ chatty services, cross-cloud transfers)
- **The cultural mechanism** — unit economics (cost per customer/transaction) in product conversations; cost regressions reviewed like performance regressions

**Follow-ups you should expect:**
- "What do you NOT cut?" — observability headroom, redundancy for Tier-1 services, and security controls — say so explicitly
- "How do you prevent teams from gaming allocations?" — enforced tagging, allocation from metered data where possible

**Common pitfall:** listing discounts (RIs/Spot) without the visibility/governance layer — allocation is what makes every other lever work.
</Step>
</Steps>
