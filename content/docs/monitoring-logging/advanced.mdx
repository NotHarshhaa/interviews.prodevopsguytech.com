# Advanced Level

This section covers expert-level monitoring and logging concepts commonly asked in interviews for senior professionals.

## Prometheus at Scale

<Steps>
<Step>
**How do you scale Prometheus for a large, multi-cluster environment?**

Prometheus is single-node by design — scale by decomposing:

- **Split by function** — separate Prometheus servers per cluster/team/region, each scraping a shard of targets (path filters, hashmod sharding on a label)
- **Federation** — a top-level Prometheus scrapes *aggregated* series (`/federate`) from child servers — good for a global overview, not for raw high-cardinality data
- **Long-term storage** — Thanos sidecars (blocks to object storage + global query + dedup) or remote_write into **Mimir/Cortex** (horizontally scalable, multi-tenant) or **VictoriaMetrics** (high compression, single-binary options)
- **The receiver pattern** — Mimir/Loki-style: agents remote_write to a scalable cluster; query there instead of fanning out per-server

The sizing rules to state: one Prometheus comfortably handles roughly ~1–5 million active series and 15s scrapes on decent hardware (memory is the binding constraint); beyond that, shard. And decide retention deliberately: 15d local + 13mo in object storage is a common, cost-sane split.
</Step>

<Step>
**How does Prometheus handle missing, stale, or dead targets?**

Four mechanisms, each for a different failure mode:

- **`up` metric** — every scrape sets `up{job,instance}` to 1 or 0; the simplest health signal (`up == 0` for 5m = target down)
- **Stale markers** — when a target disappears from discovery (pod deleted), Prometheus writes staleness markers so its series stop matching queries instantly — preventing "frozen last value" lies
- **`absent()` / `absent_over_time()`** — for series that simply never appear (a process that never started, a job that stopped exporting): `absent(up{job="critical-service"})` fires when the series is missing entirely — the alert for "the thing that was supposed to report isn't reporting"
- **Dead man's switch** — the inverse watch: a always-firing `Watchdog` alert routed to an external heartbeater (PagerDuty heartbeat, healthchecks.io); *if the heartbeat stops*, the external system pages — catching the worst failure mode: **Prometheus itself being down**, which Prometheus cannot alert on by definition

The senior insight: absent() + watchdog alerts close the "monitoring the monitor" gap — the most commonly missed layer in self-managed stacks.
</Step>

<Step>
**What is the Prometheus WAL and TSDB, and how do you operate them?**

- **WAL (write-ahead log)** — incoming samples append to WAL files (`data/wal/`) *before* being persisted into 2-hour TSDB **blocks**; on crash, Prometheus replays the WAL — covering the gap between memory and durable blocks. Checkpoints every 2 hours trim it
- **TSDB blocks** — immutable, compressed chunks (2h each) with an index; old blocks compact and (with Thanos/Mimir) upload to object storage

Operationally:

- **Disk headroom** — WAL replay after a crash is I/O-heavy; keep headroom and never run Prometheus on slow disks
- **The classic incident** — unclean shutdown → long WAL replay → monitoring "down" for many minutes; mitigated by frequent block persistence (2h default) and, for HA, redundant replicas
- **Retention** — `--storage.tsdb.retention.time` governs local blocks; compaction merges 2h blocks upward; corruption issues surface at startup — know `tsdb-util`/backup strategies or push to remote storage so local loss isn't fatal
</Step>

<Step>
**Histograms vs summaries in Prometheus — how do you choose?**

Both measure distributions (latency, sizes), and the difference determines whether you can aggregate across instances:

- **Histogram** — client counts samples into **predefined buckets** (`le="0.1"`, `le="0.5"`...). Server-side quantiles computed via `histogram_quantile()` — **aggregatable across all instances**:
  ```text
  histogram_quantile(0.95, sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])) by (le))
  ```
  Costs: bucket config is fixed at instrument time; quantiles are approximations of bucket boundaries
- **Summary** — client computes exact quantiles (φ-quantiles like p95) itself via streaming. Accurate per-instance, but **cannot be aggregated** — `avg` of two summaries' p95 is mathematically meaningless
- **Client cost** — summaries do the math on every observation; histograms are cheaper but produce more series (one per bucket)

The rule to state: **histograms by default** in distributed systems (the p95 across your fleet matters, not per-instance); summaries only for single-instance exact quantiles. Also name the unit discipline: seconds (base units) in `http_request_duration_seconds`, and consistent buckets across services so percentile aggregation is valid.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you secure Prometheus and its endpoints?**

- **The exposed-metrics reality** — `/metrics` often leaks internal topology, versions, and sometimes secrets-bearing labels. Prometheus itself has no built-in auth — harden the perimeter:
  - **Network** — scrape traffic on a private network; Prometheus reachable only from dashboards/jump hosts; NetworkPolicies in K8s
  - **Reverse proxy** — basic auth/OAuth/TLS in front of Prometheus and exporters (nginx, Traefik), or the `web.config.file` TLS/basic-auth support
- **Federation/remote_write** — TLS + auth on every remote endpoint (`--web.config.file` with TLS client certs)
- **Kubernetes** — NetworkPolicies restricting scrape sources; RBAC on the Prometheus Operator CRDs; secrets (`bearer_token_file`) for scrape auth
- **What metrics leak** — audit exported paths (`/metrics` of your apps), scrub sensitive labels at instrument time, and remember Alertmanager webhooks also carry data

The design question to anticipate: "who else can read our metrics?" — metrics reveal business volume (sales/sec), topology, and error patterns; treat them like semi-internal data.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you monitor Prometheus itself (meta-monitoring)?**

The monitoring system is itself critical infrastructure — monitor it with the same rigor:

- **Self-metrics** — Prometheus exposes its own `/metrics`: scrape health (`prometheus_tsdb_head_series` cardinality, `prometheus_target_interval_length_seconds` scrape jitter), rule evaluation cost, WAL/compaction health
- **Key health signals** — head series count trend (cardinality creep — the slow OOM), scrape duration vs interval (targets timing out), rule group evaluation duration, remote_write queue failures (data silently lost)
- **Dashboards + alerts** — the community Prometheus-self dashboard; alert on: high cardinality growth rate, evaluation failures, `prometheus_config_last_reload_successful == 0`, disk filling
- **The second level** — a *separate* lightweight Prometheus (or an external synthetics service) watching the main one, plus the **dead man's switch** (Watchdog → external heartbeat) covering total monitoring failure — the failure main Prometheus can never self-report

The architecture summary: meta-monitoring + watchdog = you learn about monitoring failures before users report blindness during an incident.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Grafana & Loki Questions

<Steps>
<Step>
**How does Grafana unified alerting work, and how do you design alert routing?**

Grafana 9+ unified alerting brings alert rules (for Prometheus, Loki, and other sources) into one engine:

- **Rule anatomy** — query + expression → condition → evaluation interval → **contact point** → **notification policy** (routing by labels)
- **States** — Normal → Pending (condition met, within `for`) → Alerting → (resolved) — with `NoData`/`Error` states to handle explicitly (a "No Data" alerting rule catches broken queries)
- **Routing** — labels route to contact points (Slack, PagerDuty, webhooks): `severity=critical` + `team=payments` → payments' PagerDuty; nested policies allow team-scoped sub-routes
- **Silences/maintenance** — time-bounded silences for deploys; MuteTimings for recurring windows

The design guidance: decide the **single home for alerting** (Alertmanager OR Grafana — running both duplicates pages), route by labels not per-alert config, and version alert rules as code (files/CRDs) like everything else.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do Grafana annotations and on-call workflows connect dashboards to incidents?**

- **Annotations** — vertical event markers on every time-series graph: deployments, incidents start/end, config changes. Automatically added via API from CI/CD (`curl` a POST on deploy) — so every dashboard shows "the error rate spiked exactly when build #4711 shipped"
- **The debugging acceleration** — correlation without cross-referencing: an engineer opens the dashboard, sees the annotation line at the inflection point, and starts with the right change
- **Grafana OnCall** — escalation chains, on-call schedules, and incident objects integrated beside dashboards; or feed annotations into/out of PagerDuty/Opsgenie
- **Automation to mention** — annotations from deployments (CI), from alerts (Alertmanager), from chaos experiments (each experiment marks the timeline)

The cultural point: annotation-augmented dashboards change post-incident review quality — "what changed?" is answered visually, instantly, and without tribal memory.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you run Grafana and Loki in multi-tenant, multi-team setups?**

- **Tenancy models** — folders + permissions (teams see their dashboards), organizations (harder separation), or separate stacks per business unit; Grafana Cloud/Mimir/Loki Enterprise bring native per-tenant isolation via `X-Scope-OrgID`
- **Data-source isolation** — per-team data sources with permissions; in multi-tenant Loki/Mimir, tenants share infrastructure but never see each other's data
- **Provisioning at fleet scale** — everything as YAML/CRDs; golden dashboards templated per service; teams extend rather than fork (or dashboards fork and drift)
- **Resource governance** — Loki per-tenant stream/ingest limits (one noisy tenant can degrade shared Loki), Grafana query concurrency/timeout limits, alert-rule evaluation caps

The scaling insight to deliver: multi-tenancy failures in monitoring are shared-fate failures — one team's cardinality explosion or heavy dashboard query degrades *everyone's* visibility, so per-tenant limits and isolation are reliability features, not niceties.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Elasticsearch & OpenTelemetry Questions

<Steps>
<Step>
**What is the hot-warm-cold architecture in Elasticsearch, and how do you implement it?**

The tiered-data strategy: logs age from expensive to cheap infrastructure as access frequency drops:

- **Hot nodes** — NVMe/SSD, high memory; actively written and queried (today's indices); `data_hot` role
- **Warm nodes** — cheaper storage; older indices still queried occasionally; `data_warm`, forcemerged single-segment indices
- **Cold/frozen** — searchable snapshots on object storage (S3); queryable but slow to restore; `data_cold`/`data_frozen`
- **ILM wires the movement** — `allocate` actions move indices by age (`min_age: 7d` → warm, `30d` → cold, `90d` → delete), using index templates to assign ILM policies automatically

The cost math to state: hot storage is often 5–10× the cost of cold — tiering cuts the logging bill dramatically while keeping the retention window auditors ask for. Requirement to mention: dedicated tier node roles and ILM policies are part of cluster setup from day one — retrofitting tiers onto an over-sharded cluster is painful.
</Step>

<Step>
**How does Logstash handle backpressure and reliability (persistent queues, DLQ)?**

The pipeline between shippers and Elasticsearch must survive ES outages and spikes without losing logs:

- **Persistent queues** — `queue.type: persisted` writes events to disk before processing: an ES outage becomes "Logstash buffers" instead of "logs lost"; `queue.max_bytes` bounds disk usage — when full, backpressure propagates to inputs (Beats retry)
- **Dead letter queues (DLQ)** — events rejected by Elasticsearch (mapping conflicts, malformed docs) route to a DLQ file for reprocessing instead of vanishing
- **Ack/retry chain** — Filebeat → Logstash → ES: each hop acknowledges; failures retry with backoff; Beasts keep a registry of shipped offsets
- **The alternative topology** — Kafka between shippers and Logstash (or ES ingest): Kafka *is* the buffer — better for extreme scale and multi-consumer fan-out; Logstash then becomes a consumer group member

The design question to anticipate: "what happens to logs during a 2-hour Elasticsearch outage?" — with persistent queues sized correctly: delayed, not lost. That answer separates designed pipelines from default configs.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you manage Elasticsearch query performance and caching?**

- **Shard request cache** — caches shard-level results for frequently-run identical queries; effective for dashboard time-windows on immutable (rolled-over) indices — size via `indices.requests.cache.size`
- **OS page cache** — the real workhorse: Lucene reads from the filesystem cache; more RAM (beyond JVM heap) = faster queries; keep heap ≤ ~50% of RAM, leave the rest to the OS cache
- **Query hygiene** — avoid leading wildcards (`*error*` scans everything), prefer `term` over `match` for keywords, use date-scoped queries (narrow indices via aliases), and paginate with `search_after` not deep `from/size`
- **Doc values / fielddata** — sorting/aggregations read doc values (columnar, on disk) by default; enabling `fielddata` on text fields loads them into heap — the classic OOM mistake
- **Instrumentation** — slow query logs (`index.search.slowlog`) expose the offending dashboards/queries; profile API for deep dives

The operation pattern: dashboards drive most load — tune the queries dashboards run (recorded ranges, fixed intervals, cached aggregations) before scaling the cluster.
</Step>

<Step>
**How does Kibana's ML-based anomaly detection work?**

Elastic's machine-learning features (X-Pack ML) run unsupervised models over your data to detect anomalies without hand-written thresholds:

- **Data frames + detectors** — a job defines functions over fields in buckets (`mean(cpu_usage)` per 15m bucket) over a training window; the model learns normal behavior per entity (per-server, per-service) including seasonality (daily/weekly cycles)
- **Anomaly scores** — each bucket gets a probability-based score; unusual values (a traffic spike at 3 a.m., a normally-quiet endpoint erroring) surface with influential contributors
- **Feeds into alerting** — ML results trigger watcher/Kibana rules ("anomaly score > 90 on checkout latency") — catching previously-unknown failure modes threshold alerts miss
- **SIEM layer** — the Kibana Security/SIEM app applies the same ML to security event streams (impossible travel, rare processes) plus detection rules

The honest framing to include: ML detects *novel* anomalies that fixed thresholds can't — but it also produces false positives during legitimate behavior changes (deploys, marketing spikes), so baselines need retraining windows and the alert routing needs the same fatigue discipline as threshold alerting.
</Step>

<Step>
**What is OpenTelemetry, and how does it change the observability stack?**

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the vendor-neutral standard for emitting **metrics, logs, and traces** — SDKs/APIs for instrumenting code, plus the **Collector**: a deployable pipeline (agent per node + central gateway) that receives (OTLP, and legacy formats), processes (filter, sample, redact, enrich), and exports to *any* backend:

- **The problem it solves** — instrumentation lock-in: code instrumented once (OTel SDK, or zero-code auto-instrumentation agents for Java/Python/Node/.NET) exports to Prometheus, Datadog, Elastic, Grafana — switching backends doesn't mean re-instrumenting the fleet
- **Collector as the pipeline** — the single place for log/metric/trace processing that Logstash-style tools used to own: tail-based sampling (keep all error traces, 1% of healthy ones), PII scrubbing, k8s metadata enrichment, multi-backend fan-out
- **Semantic conventions** — standardized names/attributes (`http.request.duration`, `service.name`) so dashboards and queries work across services and languages
- **The architecture pattern** — app → OTel agent (sidecar/DaemonSet) → OTel gateway (central collector) → backends; traces/metrics/logs flowing through one pipeline with consistent correlation IDs

The strategic answer to give: OTel is to observability what Terraform is to infrastructure — an abstraction layer that makes the backend a decision you can revisit. New instrumentation should be OTel-first; legacy agents (Promtail, Beats, StatsD) get replaced at the collector boundary.
</Step>

<Step>
**How do you control the cost of an observability stack at scale?**

Observability is often the most expensive "supporting" platform — and its costs are controllable with deliberate policy:

- **Metrics** — cardinality governance (label allowlists in instrumentation review, card-buffer alerts in Prometheus), scrape interval tuning (30s for low-value targets), recording rules + downsampling for old data, retention tiers (15d hot, 13mo cold object storage)
- **Logs** — tiered retention (hot 7–14d, cold to cheap storage), structured logging with `level`-based routing (debug logs opt-in per service, sampled), drop/filter noisy events at the collector (health-check spam), volume quotas per team
- **Traces** — head or tail-based **sampling** (keep 100% of errors/slow traces, sample the rest — tail sampling at the OTel collector), short hot retention
- **Backends** — the tier decision (Loki vs Elasticsearch, Mimir vs vanilla Prometheus) is a cost decision; object-storage backends separate compute from storage growth
- **Governance** — per-team dashboards of their own observability spend; ingestion limits enforced at the collector (a runaway debug log is an outage of the logging platform itself)

The line that lands: observability cost scales with *retention × cardinality × verbosity* — set all three deliberately per data class, instead of paying default-everything forever.
</Step>
</Steps>
